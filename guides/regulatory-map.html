Global Regulations & Standards at a Glance

Regulators in every major market now expect medical devices to be secure, and standards describe how to get there. Use this map to recognize the names, see which are binding, and know who to ask. You don't need to memorize it.

United States · FDA

Section 524B, FD&C ActLawSince March 2023

Every cyber device submission must include a plan to monitor and address postmarket vulnerabilities, with coordinated vulnerability disclosure (CVD); processes that give reasonable assurance of security, including regular updates and fast patches for critical problems; and a software bill of materials (SBOM). Since October 2023, submissions missing this can be refused.

Premarket cybersecurity guidanceGuidance2023; updated 2025; reissued Feb 2026

A Secure Product Development Framework (SPDF) inside the quality system; threat modeling and security risk management linked to safety risk; architecture views; security controls; testing; an SBOM with support status; labeling; a cybersecurity management plan.

Postmarket cybersecurity guidanceGuidanceDec 2016; still current

Monitor vulnerabilities and judge whether the risk of patient harm is controlled (fix in routine updates) or uncontrolled (fix promptly; generally reportable, with reporting relief when customers are told quickly, the fix is fast, and the manufacturer takes part in an information sharing and analysis organization).

QMSR (Quality Management System Regulation)RegulationEffective Feb 2026

Incorporates ISO 13485. No cyber clause, but FDA maps security work onto design, risk management, supplier controls and CAPA.

AI-enabled device software guidanceDraftJan 2025

For AI devices, covers AI-specific threats such as poisoned training data and manipulated inputs.

Cyber device (Section 524B): includes software, can connect to the internet, and could be vulnerable to cyber threats. FDA reads this broadly: an indirect path through a phone, transmitter, hospital network or service port can count.

European Union

MDR and IVDR: general safety and performance requirements (GSPRs)RegulationIn force

Software developed to the state of the art, with risk management that includes information security; minimum IT security requirements stated in the instructions for use; protection, as far as possible, against unauthorized access.

MDCG 2019-16GuidanceRev.1, July 2020

How to meet the GSPRs: secure design, operating environment, testing, postmarket vigilance, and joint responsibility with the hospitals that run devices.

Cyber Resilience Act (CRA)RegulationReporting Sep 2026; full Dec 2027

Excludes MDR and IVDR devices, but reaches suppliers' software components, gateways and non-medical accessories sold in the EU.

NIS2 DirectiveDirectiveNational laws from Oct 2024

About the company, not the product: organizational cyber risk management and incident reporting for manufacturers above size thresholds.

AI ActRegulationDevice AI duties moved to 2028

For AI in devices: accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity, including resistance to poisoned data and manipulated inputs.

MDR and IVDR revisionProposalUnder negotiation

Would make cybersecurity explicit in the GSPRs and add reporting of actively exploited vulnerabilities, in line with the CRA.

Other major markets and IMDRF

Canada · Health CanadaGuidance2019

Cyber evidence in higher-class licence applications: risk analysis, a bill of materials, testing, labeling, a traceability matrix and a plan for monitoring new threats.

United Kingdom · MHRADraft2026 amendments

Draft amendments would align UK requirements with the EU GSPRs; MHRA runs a cybersecurity workstream for software and AI.

Australia · TGARegulation GuidanceGuidance 2022

Manage cyber risk across the lifecycle and monitor new vulnerabilities, to meet the Essential Principles.

Japan · MHLW/PMDARegulationMandatory since Apr 2024

Cyber risk management under the Essential Principles; conformance to JIS T 81001-5-1 (identical to IEC 81001-5-1) is the accepted route.

China · NMPAGuidance2022 revision

Cybersecurity documentation for registration, update management, a component list, vulnerability assessment and testing.

IMDRF · International Medical Device Regulators ForumShared principles2020; 2023

Its 2020 cybersecurity principles are the baseline (lifecycle, shared responsibility, coordinated disclosure, SBOMs, legacy devices); 2023 documents add detail on legacy devices and SBOMs. Not law, but national rules borrow from it.

Also moving: Brazil (a non-binding guide), Singapore (a voluntary cybersecurity label for devices), South Korea (a cybersecurity review guideline). One market's approval doesn't cover another, but good evidence travels.

The good news: expectations converge

Almost every regulator asks for: security built into design, inside the quality system · security risk linked to safety risk · security testing · an SBOM · a plan for vulnerabilities after release · clear security information for customers.

Standards and frameworks · most are voluntary, but regulators recognize many

ISO 13485Requiredin the US, via the QMSR

Quality management system standard; security processes live inside it. Used by: Quality; every team

ISO 14971Voluntary

Safety risk management; its scope includes data and systems security. Used by: Risk, quality, clinical

ANSI/AAMI SW96VoluntaryFDA-recognized

Security risk management run alongside ISO 14971 (2023). Used by: Product security, risk

AAMI TIR57, TIR97Voluntary

Security risk reports; SW96 has largely replaced TIR57. TIR97 covers postmarket. Used by: Product security, PSIRT

IEC 81001-5-1VoluntaryFDA-recognized; Japan's route

Security activities across the health software lifecycle, from design to testing and updates (2021). Used by: Engineering, product security

IEC 62443-4-1, 4-2Voluntarysupports device claims

Industrial standards for secure development and component security requirements. Used by: Engineering, suppliers

ISO/IEC 29147, 30111Voluntaryreferenced by FDA

Vulnerability disclosure (receiving, publishing) and vulnerability handling (investigating, fixing). Used by: PSIRT

SPDX, CycloneDXAn SBOM isRequiredin the US; the format is your choice

Machine-readable SBOM formats that tools check against vulnerability databases. Used by: Engineering build tools

CVSS, VEXVoluntary

CVSS scores severity (FDA qualified a MITRE rubric for clinical use); VEX says whether a known flaw affects a product. Used by: PSIRT; customers read VEX

MDS2 formCustomernot a regulation

Manufacturer Disclosure Statement for Medical Device Security; hospitals compare devices with it. Used by: Hospital buyers; sales

HSCC JSP2, MC2Voluntary

Health Sector Coordinating Council: Joint Security Plan (secure-by-design framework) and Model Contract (who owns which cyber duties). Used by: Program leads; sales, procurement

NIST SSDFVoluntary

Secure Software Development Framework: secure development practices. Used by: Software engineering

CISA Secure by DesignVoluntary

Principles and a pledge for software vendors generally; not device law. Used by: Software vendors

ISAOs (Health-ISAC)Voluntaryeases FDA reporting terms

Information Sharing and Analysis Organizations share threat information. Used by: PSIRT

What this means for a product team

  • Build one program on recognized standards; the same evidence feeds FDA, EU and other submissions.
  • Security deliverables are schedule items: a US cyber device submission without them can be refused.
  • Know what applies to what: FDA rules and the MDR/IVDR cover the product; NIS2 the company; the CRA mostly suppliers; the AI Act AI features.
  • Ask regulatory which markets you sell in and which standards you declare. Current as of October 2026; not legal or regulatory advice.
