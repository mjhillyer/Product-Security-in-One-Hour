Product Security in One Hour · Reference guide 01

Meet the Devices

Four fictional products appear throughout the course. Each is a different kind of medical device with a different set of security challenges. Names, companies and details are invented; any resemblance to real products is coincidental.

In the sketches, marks parts the manufacturer builds and dashed lines mark places where the device depends on something the manufacturer doesn't control.

Implantable · home use

CardiaPace

An implantable pacemaker, a bedside home transmitter, and a cloud remote-monitoring portal used by cardiology clinics.

Why security matters here

  • Life-sustaining therapy: integrity and availability come first.
  • Implant firmware is hard to update, and patients can't simply switch it off.
  • The transmitter lives on a home network the manufacturer doesn't control.
  • One cloud portal holds data for many patients across many clinics.
Hospital connected device

FlowGuard

A fleet of large-volume infusion pumps on hospital Wi-Fi, a drug-library server, and integration with the hospital's electronic health record (EHR) for auto-programming.

Why security matters here

  • Hundreds of units on a network the hospital runs, not the manufacturer.
  • A shared drug library: one bad update can affect every pump at once.
  • Hospital security teams ask hard questions before they buy (questionnaires, the MDS2 form).
  • Downtime means nurses fall back to manual programming.

Using the cast

When you meet a new idea, ask how it would look for each device. The same principle, such as “design for updates”, plays out very differently for an implant, a hospital fleet, a phone app and a 15-year capital system. Then ask the same question of your own product.

Software as a Medical Device (SaMD)

DermaLens

An AI-assisted skin-lesion triage app: a phone camera app and a cloud model that suggests whether a lesion needs urgent dermatology review. Sold to clinics and, in some markets, used by patients.

Why security matters here

  • Runs on phones and cloud services the manufacturer doesn't fully control.
  • Many open-source and third-party components, so the SBOM matters.
  • Frequent releases: security has to keep pace with development.
  • A tampered model or image pipeline gives wrong triage advice.
Surgical · capital equipment

OrionArm

A surgical robotic system: a surgeon console running a commercial operating system, a patient-side arm cart, a vision tower, and remote service access for diagnostics and updates. Expected service life about 15 years.

Why security matters here

  • A 15-year life outlasts operating-system and third-party support: legacy risk.
  • Remote service access is powerful, so it needs strong control and logging.
  • Downtime cancels surgeries: availability is a clinical issue.
  • Updates need validation and scheduling with the hospital.
