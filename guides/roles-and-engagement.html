Roles & When to Engage Product Security

Product security is not one team's job. Your product security team can't see every design decision, customer conversation or supplier notice. The program works when each role knows its part and when to hand over.

Who does what

A typical split; your own procedure defines the real one (in a small company one person may cover three rows). Most problems happen at hand-offs, when information one role holds never reaches the role that needs it.

Patient safety lens. Security decisions are often clinical: taking FlowGuard pumps off the network, or updating a CardiaPace implant, affects patients. Clinical colleagues belong in the room.
Map your program with a RACI chart: who is Responsible, Accountable (exactly one role), Consulted and Informed for each activity? Gaps become visible. Start from the program RACI template.

Everyday habits

Ask the security question. In every design review: does this change how the product connects, what it trusts or what data it holds?
Keep the inventory honest. Report component, version and supplier changes, so a new vulnerability can be checked in hours.
No secrets in the wrong place. Keep passwords, keys and patient data out of tickets, chat, email and code.
No “temporary” back doors. Track debug ports, test accounts and default passwords; remove them before release.
Answer from approved sources. Use published security documents; route new questions. Never guess or promise a fix date.
Take field signals seriously. An odd connection, unknown setting or strange returned unit is worth a message. Report; don't reset.

When to engage your product security team

Your product security team would rather hear about ten things that turn out fine than miss one that doesn't.

Design and build

Changes to the product

  • A new interface: Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, USB, cloud, app, service port.
  • A new or changed third-party or open-source component, even a version upgrade.
  • A change to security controls: logins, permissions, encryption, updates.
  • New data types or cloud services.
Usually: early, at the next design review
Customers and field

Signals from outside

  • A customer security questionnaire, tender or contract clause.
  • A suspected vulnerability, from anyone.
  • Odd field behavior: unexpected traffic, unknown settings.
  • A complaint that might be security-related.
Usually: now, the same day
Lifecycle

Decisions about the future

  • End-of-support decisions for the product or anything in it, such as its operating system.
  • Operating-system or platform upgrade plans.
  • Changes to remote service access: tools, people, partners, ways in.
Usually: early, at the next planning milestone

Now (the same day)

Anything suggesting a product may be vulnerable today: a suspected vulnerability, odd field behavior, a possibly security-related complaint, a supplier flaw notice. Questionnaires too: they have deadlines.

Early: next design review

Planned changes to interfaces, components, data, cloud or remote access, and end-of-support dates, before the design is settled. A “minor library update” still changes the SBOM.

No need

Changes that can't affect how the product resists or recovers from attack, such as brochure photos. When in doubt, ask: it costs one message.

Reporting a suspected vulnerability or incident

You don't need to know whether it's a vulnerability (a weakness someone could exploit) or an incident (one that is, or may have been, exploited), or whether it's real. Notice and report.

  1. Report internally right away, usually to your PSIRT (product security incident response team), and to the complaint process if a customer reported it. No channel? Tell your manager the same day. Note product, version, what, where, when, who.
  2. Don't investigate or test on your own. It can disrupt therapy and destroy evidence.
  3. Preserve information. Keep emails, screenshots and logs. Don't reset, wipe or ship back a device until the team says so.
  4. Keep it confidential. Thank the reporter and say it has been passed on. Don't confirm, deny, debate or promise dates.
Patient safety first. If a patient may be affected now, follow your complaint and adverse-event process immediately and report the security angle in parallel. Never “test the theory” on a device connected to a patient.
If an outside researcher contacts you: thank them, point them to your company's published vulnerability disclosure policy (VDP), and pass everything to your PSIRT the same day. Researchers who report are helping. The goal is coordinated vulnerability disclosure: the fix and the news arrive together.

What typically happens next (your PSIRT coordinates the other teams)

1. IntakeLogs the report, acknowledges it, asks for details.
2. TriageIs it real? Which products and versions?
3. Assess riskHow exploitable? What could happen to patients?
4. Fix and communicateUpdate or mitigate; tell customers, regulators if needed, and the reporter.

You may not hear every detail afterward: information is need-to-know until a fix is ready. That is normal, not a sign your report was ignored.
