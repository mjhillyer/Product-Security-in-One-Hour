Secure by Design

Security works best, and costs least, when it is part of a device's design from the first sketch. This module covers the principles, the risk thinking and the trade-offs behind that idea.

In this module

  • Why security built in early works better, and costs less, than security added late
  • The core secure design principles, with a device example of each
  • Threat modeling and security risk in plain terms, and FDA's security control categories
  • Third-party and open-source software, and the software bill of materials (SBOM)
  • Where security has to be balanced against usability, emergency access, workflow and validation

Built in, not bolted on

Secure by design means treating security as a property of the design, like safety or reliability, rather than something added once the product works. As Module 1 showed, changing a released device is slow, validated work, and some devices can't be changed without a clinic visit.

FDA calls the organized way of doing this a Secure Product Development Framework (SPDF): a set of processes, built into the quality management system, that bring security into every phase of development and keep it going after release. It isn't a separate bureaucracy. It adds security to the design controls, reviews and risk files your teams already use.

Requirements

Security requirements sit beside functional ones.

Design

The architecture is threat-modeled while it is still a sketch.

Build and verify

Secure coding, careful choice of components, and security testing, including penetration testing (experts trying to break in).

Release and beyond

An SBOM, vulnerability monitoring and updates, covered in Module 4.

Regulatory link

FDA's premarket cybersecurity guidance recommends an SPDF built into the quality management system. The international standard IEC 81001-5-1, which FDA recognizes, describes security activities across the health software lifecycle. Module 3 covers both.

Why late fixes cost more

A flaw found in a design review costs a conversation. Found by a penetration tester just before submission, it means rework, re-testing and perhaps a delayed launch. Found in the field, it means validating a fix, getting it onto every unit, telling customers, and sometimes a clinic visit for every patient. Some decisions, such as whether CardiaPace's radio link checks who is sending commands, are nearly irreversible once devices are implanted.

From the record

In March 2019, FDA issued a safety communication about the Conexus wireless protocol used by certain Medtronic implantable defibrillators, programmers and home monitors. The protocol had no authentication, authorization or encryption, though using the weakness required short radio range. Because the gap was in the protocol itself, patches rolled out model by model at routine office visits, with the public advisory still being updated in 2021. No patient harm was reported.

Core design principles

A handful of principles show up in almost every secure device, from implant firmware to cloud services. You already know several from everyday life: a hotel key card that opens only your room is least privilege; a vault inside a locked bank is defense in depth.

PrincipleIn plain termsDevice example
Least privilegeEvery person and component gets only the access it needs.DermaLens reviewers can view triage results but not change the model.
Defense in depthSeveral independent layers, so one failure doesn't expose the patient.FlowGuard's layers, shown in the figure below.
Secure by defaultShip in the safest configuration; customers opt in to riskier options.OrionArm ships with remote service off and no shared factory password.
Minimize attack surfaceRemove every port, service and feature the product doesn't need. The attack surface is every way in.Production CardiaPace transmitters have the debug port disabled.
Fail safeWhen something goes wrong, the device falls back to a state that keeps the patient safe.A FlowGuard pump that can't reach its server keeps the last good drug library.
Authenticate and authorizeCheck who is connecting (authentication) and what they may do (authorization).A CardiaPace implant obeys only an authorized clinic programmer.
Protect integritySoftware, settings and data can't be changed undetected. A signed update carries a digital signature the device checks before installing.FlowGuard pumps reject unsigned firmware or drug libraries.
Log and detectRecord security-relevant events so problems can be noticed and investigated.OrionArm logs who used remote service, when, and what changed.
Design for updatesPlan from day one how fixes will reach every device, safely, for its whole service life.CardiaPace implant firmware needs a validated update path through the transmitter or clinic.
Patient safety lens

For a medical device, “fail safe” means therapy continues safely, not that the device locks up. A pump that stops infusing because a security certificate expired can harm a patient as surely as an attack. Good designs block unsafe changes, keep safe therapy running, and tell the clinician.

Defense in depth, in a picture

No single control is perfect: passwords leak and software has bugs. Defense in depth stacks independent layers, so an attacker who gets past one still faces the next.

From the record

In 2015, FDA issued a safety communication about the Hospira Symbiq infusion system after a researcher reported that an unauthorized user could reach it over the hospital network and change the dose. FDA strongly encouraged hospitals to move to other pumps; interim steps included closing two older remote-access services and changing a default password. No adverse events were known. Unneeded services and default passwords are what the principles above prevent.

Threat modeling and security risk

Principles say what good looks like; threat modeling shows where to apply them. Threat modeling is a structured look at a design that asks plain questions: How does this product work, and what does it connect to? What could someone do to it on purpose? What will we do about it? It works best as a team exercise (engineers, product, clinical, product security), started while the architecture is on a whiteboard and updated as the design changes.

Rate exploitability, not probability

Safety risk management estimates the probability of harm, often from failure rates. Attacks don't follow failure rates. Someone who finds a weakness can use it again and again, and once a method is published, anyone can. So security risk assessment rates exploitability instead (how easy a weakness is to use, given the access, skill and tools needed) together with the severity of the effect on patients. “No attacks have been reported” is not a reason to rate a risk low. Risks that could lead to harm feed the safety risk file, as in Module 1.

Worked example: OrionArm

The team finds that someone with stolen service credentials could connect remotely and change the console software. That is reachable with only a password (high exploitability) and could affect a surgery (high severity). The response becomes testable security requirements, such as: “Remote service sessions shall require multi-factor authentication (a second proof beyond a password) and approval by an on-site user.”

FDA's security control categories

FDA's premarket guidance recommends that a design address these categories of security control, and that the submission show how. They make a useful checklist in any market.

Control categoryThe design questionExample
AuthenticationWho or what is connecting?A clinic programmer proves itself to a CardiaPace implant.
AuthorizationCan each user do only what it should?Only pharmacy roles can publish a FlowGuard drug library.
CryptographyAre we using well-established methods?Standard encryption for DermaLens uploads.
IntegrityWould we detect unauthorized changes?Signed OrionArm software updates.
ConfidentialityIs sensitive data seen only by the right people?Encrypted patient data in the CardiaPace cloud portal.
Event detection and loggingWould we, or the customer, notice an attack?OrionArm remote-session logs.
Resiliency and recoveryDoes it keep working safely, and recover?FlowGuard keeps the last good drug library.
UpdatabilityCan we deliver fixes to every unit in time?A validated update path for CardiaPace transmitters.
Regulatory link

FDA expects threat modeling, security risk management linked to safety risk management, and architecture views showing where controls sit. ANSI/AAMI SW96 describes the security risk process.

Third-party software and the SBOM

Modern devices are largely assembled: most of the code in a typical product was written by someone else. It may be commercial (licensed from a vendor, like OrionArm's console operating system), open-source (published for anyone to use, like many of DermaLens's image libraries) or another off-the-shelf component. Whatever the source, you inherit its weaknesses along with its features, and you are responsible for it in your product.

So choosing a component is a security decision. Is it maintained? Will it be supported as long as our product? Can we update it in the field? OrionArm's 15-year service life outlasts most operating systems' support, so the team needs a plan from the start. Open source is neither automatically safe nor automatically risky: all components need the same questions.

A software bill of materials (SBOM) is the product's ingredients list: every software component with its name, version and supplier, plus, as FDA expects, its support status and end-of-support date. It is usually generated by build tools in a machine-readable format such as SPDX or CycloneDX. When a flaw is announced in a popular library, an accurate SBOM answers “which of our products are affected?” in minutes rather than weeks. For US cyber devices, the law requires an SBOM in the premarket submission.

Practical tip

When your team proposes a new component, bring three facts to the design review: its version, who supports it, and until when. If nobody knows the last answer, raise it with product security now, not in year eight.

Trade-offs unique to devices

In office IT, the answer is often “lock it down”. In a medical device, every control also has to work for a nurse at 3 a.m., a surgeon mid-procedure or a patient at home. Device teams balance security against five things.

Trade-offWhy it matters, with an example
UsabilityA control that's too hard gets worked around. Gloved nurses can't type long passwords on every FlowGuard pump, so the design might use badge taps.
Emergency accessEmergency teams must reach the device quickly. CardiaPace must stay programmable away from its usual clinic, so a design might rely on close physical proximity rather than a password nobody in the emergency room knows.
Clinical workflowUpdates and reboots have to fit the clinical day. An OrionArm update is scheduled with the hospital, never sprung between operations.
Performance and batterySecurity uses computing power and energy. On an implant, every extra calculation draws on a battery that is replaced only by surgery.
Validation burdenEvery software change needs verification and validation, sometimes regulatory review. That favors designs that make updates safe and routine.

Security controls can introduce hazards of their own: a lockout, a slow login or an update at the wrong moment can delay care. So security decisions go through the same risk management as any other design decision. Good teams make trade-offs explicitly: they involve clinical users early, look for controls that are both secure and easy, add compensating controls where a strong one isn't possible, record the rationale in the risk file, and tell customers in the labeling what to do.

Common pitfall

Adding a strong control without asking the people who will use it. A password policy that ignores the clinical day leads to shared logins and passwords taped to the device, leaving the product less secure than before. Test security features with real users.

Keep the Secure by Design guide handy in design reviews, and the Glossary for terms.

Try it≈ 4 min

Secure one feature by design

Pick one feature of your product that connects to something: remote access, an app, an update path, a cloud upload. No product to hand? Use OrionArm's remote service access, which lets the manufacturer's engineers diagnose consoles and install updates from a service center. Write down:

  1. When you would threat-model it, and one thing someone could do with it on purpose.
  2. How you would rate that risk, in terms of exploitability and severity.
  3. Three or four controls, naming the principle each one applies.
  4. One trade-off with clinical or service users, and how you would resolve it.
Compare with a worked example

Feature: OrionArm remote service access.

  1. When and what: now, while the architecture is still being drawn. Threat: someone with stolen service credentials connects to a console from anywhere and changes its software.
  2. Risk: high exploitability (reachable over the network with only a password) and high severity (it could affect a surgery). Because patient harm is possible, it also feeds the safety risk file.
  3. Controls: remote access off until the hospital turns it on (secure by default); multi-factor authentication for each named engineer, no shared password (authenticate and authorize); approval by an on-site user for each session (authorize); every session logged (log and detect); only signed software installs (protect integrity). Together they are defense in depth.
  4. Trade-off: surgeons worry the on-site approval step could delay an urgent fix on a busy day. Don't drop the step, and don't dismiss the concern. Work with clinical and service staff on a fast approval path, such as a one-tap approval at the console by the charge nurse or biomedical engineer, and record the trade-off in the risk file.

Notice what the team didn't choose: “no remote access at all” shrinks the attack surface, but over a 15-year life it makes fixes slow and costly to deliver. Each step also produced evidence that regulators and hospital customers will expect to see, as Module 4 explains.
