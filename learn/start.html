Start here

This is a free, self-paced introduction to medical device product security. In about an hour you'll learn why security is a patient-safety issue, how it's built into a device, what regulators expect, and where you fit. You don't need a security background.

By the end of the hour you will be able to

  • Explain how a security weakness in a connected device can become patient harm, sometimes for many patients at once
  • Recognize the core Secure by Design principles in real design decisions
  • Describe, at a high level, what FDA, the EU and other regulators and the key standards expect
  • Outline the product security work that happens before release, after release and at end of support
  • Know when to bring in product security and what to do if you suspect a vulnerability

How the course works

  • Six short modules after this one, each with a time estimate. They add up to about 60 minutes. Stop at any point and come back: this browser remembers which modules you've marked complete, and nothing is sent anywhere.
  • Each module ends with a “Try it” exercise and a worked example to compare against. There are no quizzes and nothing is scored.
  • Every key idea is also a printable reference guide. Download them from the reference library, one at a time or as a single pack, along with editable templates.

Pick a product of your own

Before you start, choose a product you know: one you build, sell, support or are planning. Keep it in mind as you go. Several exercises ask you to apply an idea to it, so by the end you'll have a short list of questions to take back to your team.

Good first choices

A device with a wireless link or a companion app, a product that connects to a hospital network or the cloud, software that runs on phones, or anything with a service or update path. If you don't work on a device today, use one of the four below.

Meet the four devices

The examples use four fictional products. Each represents a different kind of medical device, and each meets the same ideas in a different way: designing for updates means something very different for an implant than for a phone app.

Implantable

CardiaPace

An implanted pacemaker, a bedside home transmitter that uploads readings, and a cloud portal cardiology clinics use. Life-sustaining, hard to update, and partly on the patient's home network.

Hospital connected device

FlowGuard

A fleet of infusion pumps on hospital Wi-Fi with a shared drug-library server and EHR integration. One bad library update could affect every pump at once.

Software as a Medical Device

DermaLens

An AI-assisted skin-lesion triage app: a phone camera app plus a cloud model. Runs on phones and cloud services the manufacturer doesn't fully control.

Surgical capital equipment

OrionArm

A surgical robot with a console on a commercial operating system and remote service access, expected to stay in service for about 15 years.

The Meet the Devices guide has a sketch of each one.

The devices, and the companies behind them, are invented for teaching. A few real public events are cited briefly, from regulator and government advisories, to show that the risks are real. Regulatory content is current as of October 2026. This is educational material, not legal or regulatory advice.
