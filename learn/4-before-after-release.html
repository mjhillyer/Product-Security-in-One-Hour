Module 4 · 12 minutes

Before and after release: security across the lifecycle

A device's security isn't finished when it ships. This module follows a product through its whole life: the evidence built before release, how vulnerabilities are handled after it, and what happens when a device outlasts its software.

In this module

  • The total product lifecycle, and why its phases are linked
  • The premarket security evidence a cyber device submission typically includes
  • How a vulnerability report is handled after release, from first email to fix
  • Controlled versus uncontrolled risk, and why it changes the pace of the response
  • End of support and legacy devices, and who does what about them

One product, one long lifecycle

Regulators and industry describe device security in terms of the total product lifecycle (TPLC): every stage from the first design idea until the last unit leaves service. For DermaLens, that may mean dozens of releases in a few years; for OrionArm, about 15 years in operating rooms.

The lifecycle is usually split into four phases. Premarket covers everything before the product is authorized and sold. Release is the hand-off from building to watching. Postmarket is the product's working life, usually the longest phase. End of support is the planned exit.

Two ideas matter more than any single box. The phases are linked. A device never designed to accept updates can't be patched, and a team that never recorded what software is inside can't tell whether a new vulnerability affects it. The lifecycle is a loop. A postmarket fix or new feature is a design change, so it goes back through threat modeling, testing and documentation before it ships.

Regulatory link

The IMDRF's Principles and Practices for Medical Device Cybersecurity (2020) is built around the total product lifecycle. In the US, Section 524B ties the phases together in law: a cyber device's premarket submission must include a plan for monitoring and addressing postmarket vulnerabilities, including coordinated vulnerability disclosure, and processes for providing updates and patches.

Common pitfall

“Security is done: the submission was cleared.” Clearance starts the longest phase. New vulnerabilities turn up every week in components a product already contains.

Premarket: build it in and show the evidence

Premarket security work has two outputs: a product secure enough for its intended use, and the evidence that shows it to regulators, hospital customers and your own future colleagues.

The submission

For a cyber device in the US, the premarket submission brings this evidence together. The law requires the postmarket vulnerability plan, evidence of processes that give reasonable assurance the device is secure, and an SBOM. FDA's premarket cybersecurity guidance (reissued in February 2026) adds the rest of the table above, built inside a Secure Product Development Framework (SPDF), plus a cybersecurity management plan for after release. Other regulators ask for similar material; Health Canada explicitly asks for a traceability matrix.

Produce the evidence during design. Reconstructing it at the end is slow and usually reveals gaps when they cost most to fix.

Patient safety

Testing must also show that security controls don't block therapy. If a FlowGuard pump can't verify a new drug library's signature, it should keep infusing with the last good library and alert staff, not stop. Premarket testing proves the device fails safe.

Practical tip

Read the current MDS2 form for a product you work on as a hospital buyer would. If anything no longer matches the product, tell your product security team.

Postmarket: watch, assess, fix and tell

After release, a product's security changes even if its code doesn't: new weaknesses are found in operating systems and libraries that thousands of products share. Postmarket work catches those findings, decides what they mean for patients, fixes them and tells the right people.

Finding vulnerabilities

  • Vulnerability monitoring compares the SBOM with public vulnerability databases and supplier advisories, so the team learns quickly when a component it ships has a known flaw.
  • Coordinated vulnerability disclosure (CVD) gives outside security researchers a published, predictable way to report what they find, and an agreement to fix and disclose together rather than surprise each other.
  • Complaints and service calls. Customers rarely say “vulnerability”. Front-line teams need to recognize when odd behavior might be a security issue.

Many organizations route all of these to a product security incident response team (PSIRT), which coordinates the response.

From the record

In 2022, researchers at Rapid7 reported issues in Baxter's Sigma Spectrum infusion pump, including a Wi-Fi module that stored network credentials unencrypted and kept them after a factory reset. Disclosure was coordinated with Baxter and CISA (the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency), which published an advisory alongside a firmware fix and new decommissioning instructions in the manual. That is CVD working as intended.

Assessing risk: controlled or uncontrolled?

Not every vulnerability is an emergency. The team first confirms the issue and which versions are affected, then assesses how exploitable it is and how severe the impact on patients could be. FDA's postmarket cybersecurity guidance (2016) sorts the result into two kinds of risk:

Controlled risk

The remaining risk of patient harm is acceptable, given the controls already in place. Record the assessment and fix it in a routine, planned update.

Uncontrolled risk

The remaining risk of patient harm is unacceptable. It needs prompt remediation, faster and wider customer communication, often interim measures before a full fix, and generally a report to the regulator.

This one decision sets the pace for everything that follows. Most findings turn out to be controlled. The skill is spotting the few that aren't, without treating every finding as a crisis.

Reporting to FDA

Under FDA's postmarket policy, routine fixes for controlled risk generally aren't reportable, while fixing an uncontrolled risk generally is. FDA eases that reporting when there are no known serious adverse events or deaths, customers are told within 30 days and the problem is fixed or mitigated within 60 days, and the manufacturer takes part in an Information Sharing and Analysis Organization (ISAO), such as Health-ISAC, where manufacturers and hospitals share threat information. Other markets have their own rules; your regulatory team decides what is reportable where.

Go deeper

Teams often score vulnerabilities with CVSS; FDA qualified a MITRE rubric that adapts it to clinical context. VEX statements tell customers whether a component's known vulnerability actually affects the product. ISO/IEC 29147 and 30111 describe disclosure and handling.

Fixing and updating

Section 524B expects updates on a regular cycle for known unacceptable vulnerabilities, and out of cycle, as soon as possible, for critical ones. How fast a fix can reach patients depends on the device:

DermaLens

An app update can reach users within days.

FlowGuard

Scheduled with each hospital's biomedical engineers.

OrionArm

Planned around surgery lists.

CardiaPace

The transmitter updates itself; the implant usually needs a clinic visit.

Every update is a device change, verified and validated before release, and it only protects patients once it's installed.

Patient safety

A patch is a clinical decision as well as a technical one. Updating a pump mid-infusion, or an implant in a fragile patient, carries its own risk. When a fix will take time, interim measures, such as restricting a network connection or disabling a feature, can protect patients now, as long as they don't create a new hazard.

Telling the right people

Customer communication tells hospitals, clinicians and sometimes patients what is affected, how serious it is, what to do now and when a fix will come, coordinated with the researcher so the advisory and the fix land together. Staying silent until the patch is ready is the tempting mistake.

Incident response covers actual attacks, such as ransomware reaching OrionArm consoles in a hospital. The PSIRT works with the hospital, service, quality and regulatory teams to contain it, check patient safety and restore a known good state.

End of support and legacy devices

Every product eventually stops receiving support. End of life is when a manufacturer stops selling a product. End of support is when it stops providing service and support, including security updates. A legacy device is one that can no longer be reasonably protected against current threats, yet is often still in daily clinical use.

OrionArm shows the problem. Its surgeon console runs a commercial operating system, which will typically get security updates for far fewer years than OrionArm's 15-year service life. Without a plan, the console will one day run software nobody is fixing, on a hospital network, during surgery.

Design: plan the exit early

Choose components with long support periods, make the operating system upgradeable, and record support dates in the SBOM.

In service: tell customers in time

Announce end-of-support dates early, offer upgrade paths, and advise on compensating controls, such as network separation, that reduce risk when patching isn't possible.

Retirement: leave nothing behind

Give final updates and instructions for wiping patient data, credentials and network settings before disposal or resale.

From the record

In May 2017 the WannaCry ransomware spread worldwide and disrupted parts of England's National Health Service. The NHS England review found some diagnostic equipment affected, noted MRI scanners and blood-test analysers that “still use Microsoft XP software supplied by third parties”, and recorded slow and inadequate responses from device vendors. It reported no harm to patients. The devices were caught because of the software they ran.

Legacy risk is a shared responsibility. The IMDRF's guidance on legacy devices (2023) asks manufacturers to communicate support timelines clearly, and hospitals to keep inventories, apply compensating controls and budget for replacements. Removing a legacy device has its own clinical cost, so the goal is to keep the remaining risk visible and controlled.

Try it≈ 4 min

Handle a researcher's report

A security researcher emails the FlowGuard support inbox. She has found that FlowGuard pumps accept drug-library files that aren't signed: anyone who can reach the pumps over the hospital Wi-Fi can load a modified library, including changed dose limits, onto every pump on that network. She hasn't seen it exploited, and she plans to present her findings at a conference in four months.

1. Put these response steps in the order they normally happen.

  • A. Release the update, help hospitals deploy it, and track how many pumps are patched
  • B. Assess exploitability and the potential for patient harm, and decide whether the risk is controlled or uncontrolled
  • C. Close the loop: update the threat model, SBOM and design practices for the next release
  • D. Acknowledge the report to the researcher and open a case in the PSIRT process
  • E. Coordinate disclosure: agree timing with the researcher, publish customer guidance, and report to regulators where required
  • F. Triage: reproduce the issue and identify which FlowGuard versions and components are affected
  • G. Develop and validate a fix, plus interim measures if the fix will take time

2. Is this controlled or uncontrolled risk? Name one interim measure hospitals could take before the fix arrives.

Compare with a worked example

1. The usual order: D, F, B, G, E, A, C.

  1. D. Acknowledge and open a case. A quick reply builds trust; silence pushes researchers to publish alone.
  2. F. Triage. Confirm it's real and which versions it affects.
  3. B. Assess. This sets the pace for everything after it.
  4. G. Fix, plus interim measures. The fix is a design change, so it's validated.
  5. E. Coordinate disclosure. Advisory, fix and reports line up before the conference.
  6. A. Release and track. A fix protects no one until it's installed.
  7. C. Close the loop. The finding feeds the next design.

Steps overlap in practice: with uncontrolled risk, interim guidance often goes out before the fix.

2. Most likely uncontrolled. Exploitability is high: no credentials, reachable from the hospital network, every pump exposed at once. Impact is severe: dose limits are a safety control, so a changed library could mean wrong doses for many patients. That means prompt remediation, fast customer communication and generally a regulator report.

Interim measures: restrict the network so only the hospital's drug-library server can reach the pumps, and have biomedical engineers check each pump's library against the approved version. Taking every pump offline isn't a good default: manual programming carries its own risk.

Compare: if the flaw needed physical access to each pump, and the pump enforced hard limits no library could change, the remaining risk might be acceptable: controlled, fixed in the next planned release.
