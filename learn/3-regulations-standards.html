Regulations & standards: what the rules expect

Regulators in every major market now expect medical devices to be secure, and standards describe how to get there. This module is a map: how the pieces relate, what the main markets ask for, and what that means for your team. The goal is to recognize the names and know who to ask, not to memorize them.

Current as of October 2026

This area changes often; not legal or regulatory advice. Statuses and dates were checked in October 2026 and will move. Your regulatory team tracks the current versions for your products and owns how they apply to you.

In this module

  • How law, regulator guidance, standards and your quality system fit together, and which are binding
  • What the US FDA and the EU device regulations expect for cybersecurity, in plain terms
  • How other major markets compare, and how IMDRF keeps them aligned
  • The key standards and frameworks, and what each one is used for

Why so many rules?

Almost every market's device regulator has added cybersecurity expectations over the past decade. The rules draw on shared international principles, but differ in wording, timing and the evidence they want. A product such as FlowGuard, sold in the US, the EU and Japan, has to satisfy all three.

Regulators treat cybersecurity as part of device safety, not an IT add-on: a device that can be attacked can hurt patients, sometimes many at once (see Module 1).

Four layers: what, how, and how you do it

The documents you'll hear named come in four layers, and knowing the layer tells you how much room you have. Law must be met. Guidance isn't legally binding, but it is what reviewers look for. Standards, from bodies such as ISO, IEC and AAMI, are voluntary, but regulators often recognize them as a way to show a requirement is met. Voluntary frameworks from industry groups and government agencies sit beside them.

A law can also pull a standard in: the US quality regulation incorporates ISO 13485, so its requirements are binding for US devices.

Common pitfall: “It's only guidance”

Guidance isn't law, but it is what reviewers look for. Teams that ignore it usually end up answering the same questions later, under time pressure.

The good news: expectations converge. Almost every regulator asks for security built into design, security risk linked to safety risk, testing, a list of software components, a plan for vulnerabilities after release, and clear security information for customers.

United States

The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) regulates devices. Four documents do most of the cybersecurity work.

The law: Section 524B

Since 2023, Section 524B of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic (FD&C) Act has required every submission for a cyber device to include:

  • a plan to monitor, identify and address vulnerabilities after release, including coordinated vulnerability disclosure (CVD), a process for handling reports from outside researchers;
  • processes that give reasonable assurance the device and related systems are secure, including regular updates and fast patches for critical problems;
  • a software bill of materials (SBOM), a list of the commercial, open-source and off-the-shelf software components inside the product.

FDA reads “cyber device” broadly: any device that contains software or is software. It can refuse to accept a submission that lacks these items, so security deliverables are schedule items. If they aren't ready, the submission isn't either.

From the record

In 2019, FDA warned about URGENT/11, flaws in a third-party networking component built into several embedded operating systems that FDA said could allow remote takeover of a device. Affected products included an imaging system, an infusion pump and an anesthesia machine; there were no adverse event reports. FDA asked manufacturers to assess the risk and help hospitals find affected devices. That is only fast if you know what is inside your product, which is what an SBOM is for.

The premarket and postmarket guidance

FDA's premarket guidance, Cybersecurity in Medical Devices: Quality Management System Considerations and Content of Premarket Submissions, explains the evidence FDA expects to see: a Secure Product Development Framework (SPDF) in the quality system, threat modeling and security risk management linked to safety risk, architecture views, security controls, testing including penetration testing, an SBOM with each component's support status, and security labeling. The 2025 update added detail on Section 524B; the February 2026 reissue aligned it with the new quality regulation. For AI products such as DermaLens, FDA's 2025 draft guidance on AI-enabled devices adds threats such as poisoned training data.

The 2016 postmarket guidance is still current. When a vulnerability is found, the manufacturer judges whether the risk of patient harm is controlled or uncontrolled, based on how exploitable it is and how severe the harm could be. Controlled risks are fixed through routine updates and generally aren't reported to FDA as corrections. Uncontrolled risks must be fixed promptly and are generally reported, with lighter expectations when there is no known serious harm, customers are told quickly, the fix is fast, and the manufacturer belongs to an information sharing and analysis organization (ISAO). Module 4 covers this in practice.

A judgment about patients

The same flaw might be routine in a CardiaPace clinic report viewer and urgent in the home transmitter, because the paths to harm differ.

The quality regulation: QMSR

Since February 2026, the Quality Management System Regulation (QMSR) has incorporated the international quality standard ISO 13485. It has no cybersecurity clause, but FDA maps security work onto its processes: design, risk management, supplier controls for third-party software, and corrective and preventive action (CAPA). Security work is quality-system work.

European Union

In the EU, the Medical Device Regulation (MDR) and In Vitro Diagnostic Regulation (IVDR) are binding law. Their general safety and performance requirements (GSPRs) apply to every device. In plain terms, the manufacturer must:

  • develop software to the state of the art, with risk management that includes information security;
  • set minimum IT and IT security requirements, including protection against unauthorized access, and state them in the instructions for use;
  • protect the device, as far as possible, against unauthorized access that could stop it working as intended.

The main guidance is MDCG 2019-16. It is non-binding and explains how to meet those requirements: secure design, the operating environment, testing, postmarket vigilance, and the joint responsibility of manufacturers and the hospitals that run devices.

The wider EU digital rulebook

Four more names come up often. Keep straight what each one applies to.

Supply chain

Cyber Resilience Act (CRA)

Excludes products covered by the MDR or IVDR, so it mostly doesn't apply to the device itself. It does apply to suppliers' commercial software components, gateways and non-medical accessories sold in the EU.

Organization

NIS2 Directive

About the company, not the product. Device manufacturers above size thresholds need organizational cyber risk management, including secure development and vulnerability handling, plus incident reporting.

AI features

AI Act

For AI in devices such as DermaLens, it requires accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity, including resistance to poisoned data and manipulated inputs. Its deadlines for device AI were pushed back to 2028.

Watch this space

MDR and IVDR revision

A Commission proposal would make cybersecurity explicit in the device requirements and add reporting of actively exploited vulnerabilities, in line with the CRA. It is still under negotiation.

What it means for your role

Supply chain: ask EU suppliers how they are preparing for the CRA. Product owners: if the device uses AI, plan for the AI Act alongside the MDR. Engineers: the minimum IT security requirements you define end up in the instructions for use.

Other major markets and IMDRF

Elsewhere the details differ, but the direction is the same:

  • Canada. Health Canada's guidance expects cyber evidence in higher-class licence applications: risk analysis, a bill of materials, testing, labeling and a plan for monitoring new threats.
  • United Kingdom. Draft 2026 amendments would align UK requirements with the EU GSPRs, and the regulator, MHRA, has a cybersecurity workstream in its software and AI programme.
  • Australia. The TGA expects cyber risk to be managed across the lifecycle, with new vulnerabilities monitored, to meet its Essential Principles.
  • Japan. Cyber risk management has been mandatory since 2024. Conformance to JIS T 81001-5-1, Japan's identical version of IEC 81001-5-1, is the accepted route.
  • China. The NMPA's registration guideline expects cyber documentation, update management, a component list and vulnerability testing.

Others are moving too: Brazil has a non-binding guide, Singapore a voluntary cybersecurity label for devices, and South Korea a cybersecurity review guideline.

The International Medical Device Regulators Forum (IMDRF) is where regulators agree shared principles. Its 2020 Principles and Practices for Medical Device Cybersecurity is the baseline: a total product lifecycle view, shared responsibility, coordinated disclosure, information sharing, SBOMs and legacy devices. Documents from 2023 add detail on legacy devices and on SBOMs. IMDRF documents aren't law, but national rules borrow from them, which is why the core asks look so similar and good evidence travels well.

Go deeper

IMDRF's legacy-device document describes how manufacturers and hospitals share responsibility as a device reaches end of support. It matters most for long-lived products such as OrionArm; see Module 4.

Standards and frameworks

Standards say how. Because several regulators recognize the same ones, building a program on them is the most efficient way to serve many markets.

Lifecycle

IEC 81001-5-1

Secure development lifecycle activities for health software, from risk management and design to testing and update management.

Risk

ISO 14971, AAMI SW96, TIR57, TIR97

Safety risk management, and security risk management alongside it. SW96 has largely replaced TIR57 in practice; TIR97 covers postmarket.

Quality

ISO 13485

The quality management system standard, now part of the US QMSR. Security processes live inside it.

Capabilities

IEC 62443-4-1 and 4-2

Industrial standards for secure development and for component security requirements, often used to support device security claims.

Vulnerabilities

ISO/IEC 29147 and 30111

Receiving and publishing vulnerability information (disclosure), and investigating and fixing it (handling).

Customers

MDS2 form

A standard security disclosure form hospitals use to compare devices; FlowGuard's buyers ask for it. A customer expectation, not a regulation.

Beside them sit voluntary frameworks: the Health Sector Coordinating Council's Joint Security Plan (JSP2) for devices and health IT, and its Model Contract (MC2), which splits cyber responsibilities between manufacturers and providers; NIST's Secure Software Development Framework (SSDF); and CISA's Secure by Design principles and pledge, aimed at software vendors generally rather than device law.

Put together, a typical program keeps its procedures in an ISO 13485 quality system, manages risk with ISO 14971 and SW96, runs lifecycle activities to IEC 81001-5-1, handles vulnerabilities to ISO/IEC 29147 and 30111, and informs customers with the MDS2 and SBOM. That evidence feeds FDA, EU and other submissions.

One standard, several markets

FDA recognizes IEC 81001-5-1 and AAMI SW96, and Japan's identical JIS T 81001-5-1 is its accepted route. For machine-learning devices, FDA has also recognized AAMI CR515, a consensus report on their cybersecurity.

Practical tip: ask your regulatory team which markets you sell in and which standards you declare conformance to. Keep the reference guide handy: Global Regulations & Standards at a Glance.

Try it≈ 3 min

Map the rules for a two-market launch

The DermaLens team (an AI-assisted skin-lesion triage app: a phone camera app plus a cloud model) plans to launch a new version in the US and the EU. Using the table above, sort the rules into four buckets:

  1. The product: which rules apply to DermaLens itself in each market, and what is one thing each asks for?
  2. A feature: does anything extra apply because DermaLens uses AI?
  3. The company: which rule is about the manufacturer, not the app?
  4. Sounds relevant, but: which one doesn't apply to the device itself, and why might it still matter?

Then name the evidence that could serve both markets, and one question for your regulatory team.

Compare with a worked example

1. The product.

  • US: DermaLens is software, so FDA treats it as a cyber device and Section 524B applies: a postmarket vulnerability plan with CVD, secure update processes, and an SBOM (real work for an app with many open-source components). The premarket guidance describes the rest of the evidence, and the 2016 postmarket guidance shapes vulnerability handling after launch.
  • EU: the MDR's GSPRs apply. The software lifecycle must include information security, and the app's minimum IT security requirements (for example, supported phone operating system versions) go in the instructions for use. MDCG 2019-16 explains how.

2. A feature. In the US, FDA's 2025 draft guidance on AI-enabled devices covers threats such as poisoned training data. In the EU, the AI Act expects accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity, on the later timeline for device AI (2028). Confirm the details with regulatory.

3. The company. NIS2 may apply to the manufacturer if it is above the size thresholds. It covers the organization's security and incident reporting, not the app.

4. Sounds relevant, but. The Cyber Resilience Act excludes products covered by the MDR, so it isn't the app's cyber law. It can still reach suppliers of commercial software components the app relies on.

One body of evidence: a single secure development process in the quality system, built on IEC 81001-5-1, SW96 with ISO 14971, and ISO/IEC 29147 and 30111, packaged differently for the FDA submission and the EU technical documentation.

A question for regulatory: “Which standards do we declare conformance to, and is the company in scope of NIS2?”

Now do it for your own product: list its markets, then sort the rules the same way.
