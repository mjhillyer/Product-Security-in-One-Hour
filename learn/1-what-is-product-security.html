Module 1 · 10 minutes

What is product security?

A medical device that runs software can be attacked, and an attack on a device can hurt a patient. Product security is the work of preventing that, for as long as the device is in use.

In this module

  • Why connected medical devices need a different approach from office IT
  • How a security weakness can turn into patient harm
  • Confidentiality, integrity and availability, and why availability of therapy matters
  • What makes a product a “cyber device” under US law
  • How product security differs from enterprise IT security and from privacy

Why connected devices are different

Product security (in full, product cybersecurity) is the work of making sure a product, and everything it depends on, can resist, detect and recover from attacks for as long as it is in use. For a medical device, “everything it depends on” includes the device itself, its software and firmware, the apps and cloud services it talks to, the tools used to update and service it, and the third-party components inside all of these.

Most people meet security at work: passwords, phishing training, forced laptop updates. That is enterprise IT security, which protects an organization's own computers and data. Product security protects what the organization makes and sells, which then lives in hospitals, clinics and homes the manufacturer doesn't control. Four things make that harder for medical devices, shown here with the course's fictional devices (see Meet the Devices).

1

Patients can be harmed

When an office laptop is attacked, the usual worst case is lost data. When a therapy device is attacked, it's a patient who gets the wrong therapy, no therapy or a missed alarm.

Many patients at once

Connected devices share infrastructure: a cloud portal, an update server, a drug library. One weakness there can reach every patient using the product. This is multi-patient harm, the scenario regulators worry about most.

3

Long service lives

A phone is replaced every few years. A surgical robot may be in service for 15 years, long after its operating system stops getting security fixes.

4

Environments you don't run

Hospitals run their own networks; patients use their own phones and home Wi-Fi. The manufacturer designs for places it can't see or manage, and shares responsibility with the people who run them.

Control · DermaLens triage app

Devices also can't always be changed quickly. A software change usually has to be verified and validated, may need regulatory review, and sometimes needs a technician or clinic visit to install. An implanted pacemaker can't download a patch overnight like a phone app. That is why security has to be designed in from the start, the subject of Module 2.

Common pitfall

“The device is on the hospital's network, so the hospital's IT team handles security.” Hospitals do share the work, but they can only protect what the manufacturer makes protectable. They can't add authentication to a pump that doesn't support it, or patch an operating system the manufacturer hasn't validated. And ransomware spreading through a hospital network hits whatever it can reach, whether or not anyone was aiming at the device.

Safety and security: two sides of one goal

Device teams already know safety risk management: identify hazards, estimate the risk of harm, control it, and check the controls work. The international standard for this is ISO 14971. Security adds a new kind of cause. A hazard can now be triggered on purpose, by someone exploiting a weakness, not only by a component failure or a use error.

The chain below shows how. Security work focuses on the left side (who could attack, through which weakness, with what effect); safety work on the right (the hazardous situation and the harm). They meet in the middle, and information has to flow both ways.

Where the standards connect

ISO 14971 explicitly includes data and systems security in its scope. ANSI/AAMI SW96, a security risk management standard that FDA recognizes, describes how manufacturers run a security risk process alongside ISO 14971, so that security risks that could lead to harm flow into the safety risk file. FDA expects the two processes to be separate but linked: safety teams own hazards and harm, and security teams bring the “on purpose” causes.

Confidentiality, integrity and availability

Security people describe what they protect with three properties. For medical devices, their order of importance is often the reverse of what people expect.

PropertyWhat it meansDevice example
ConfidentialityOnly the right people and systems can see the data.DermaLens images and triage results are health data. A leak is a privacy harm and a regulatory problem.
IntegrityData, settings, software and commands can't be changed without authorization, and changes can be detected.If someone could change CardiaPace pacing settings or FlowGuard dose limits, patients could be harmed directly.
AvailabilityThe device and its functions work when they're needed.If ransomware locks the OrionArm console, the day's surgeries are postponed.

For a device that delivers therapy, availability means availability of therapy: the pump infuses, the pacemaker paces, the robot is ready for the surgery list. Not every loss of availability is an attack (a cloud outage can stop DermaLens results for a day), but the design question is the same: what happens to the patient then?

Patient safety lens

For therapy-delivering devices, integrity and availability usually matter more than confidentiality. A leaked heart-rhythm report is serious; a changed pacing setting can be fatal. That is why device security work starts from the question “what could this do to a patient?” rather than “what data could be stolen?”

What makes a product a “cyber device”?

In the United States, the law has a name for devices that need this attention. Section 524B of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act), added at the end of 2022 and in force for submissions since March 2023, defines a cyber device as one that meets all three of these conditions:

  1. It includes software that the manufacturer validated, installed or authorized, as the device or in the device.
  2. It has the ability to connect to the internet.
  3. It has technological characteristics that could be vulnerable to cybersecurity threats.

For a cyber device, the premarket submission (the application to FDA before the device can be sold) must include a plan for monitoring and addressing vulnerabilities after release, evidence of processes that give reasonable assurance the device is secure (including providing updates and patches), and a software bill of materials (SBOM): a list of the software components inside the product. Modules 3 and 4 cover these in more detail.

The second condition sounds narrow, but FDA reads it broadly. A device that can reach the internet by any path, including through a phone, a home transmitter, a hospital network, Bluetooth or a service port, may qualify. All four devices in this course would be treated as cyber devices. CardiaPace's implant never touches the internet directly, but its home transmitter does.

What it means for your role
  • Product owners and program managers: assume a new product with software is a cyber device unless regulatory concludes otherwise, and plan the security work from the start.
  • Engineers: any connection counts, including debug and service ports, so list them all.
  • Quality and regulatory: the cyber device determination and its rationale belong in the design history.

Other regions don't use the term, but reach a similar place: the EU's medical device regulations, for example, require software to be protected against unauthorized access. Module 3 gives the global tour.

Not legal advice

This course is training, not legal or regulatory advice; regulatory content is current as of October 2026. Your regulatory team owns the interpretation for your products.

The product security program

A product security program is how an organization makes all of this routine: the people, processes and tools that build security into every product and keep it there until the product is retired. It sits next to two neighbors that people often confuse with it.

Enterprise IT securityPrivacyProduct security
ProtectsThe company's own computers, networks and dataPeople's personal information and how it is usedThe products the company sells, and the patients and customers who use them
Typical workEmployee accounts, laptops, email filtering, office networksConsent, data minimization, privacy notices, data subject rightsSecure design, threat modeling, security testing, SBOMs, vulnerability handling, customer security documentation, updates
ExamplePatching the finance team's laptopsDeciding how long DermaLens keeps patient imagesMaking sure DermaLens images are encrypted and the app's components are monitored for vulnerabilities

The three overlap, so the teams work together. A simple test: if the task protects something you ship, or the patients and customers who use it, it's product security. If it protects your own offices, staff and corporate systems, it's enterprise IT. If it's about what personal data you collect and why, it's privacy.

A product security program spans the whole life of a product:

  • Before release (premarket): design security in, analyze threats and risks, define and test security requirements, and prepare what regulators and customers need to see.
  • After release (postmarket): watch for new vulnerabilities, accept reports from researchers, fix problems, ship updates, tell customers what to do, and report to regulators when required.
  • At end of support: tell customers when support ends and what that means for them.

Many organizations have a product security incident response team (PSIRT) that handles vulnerability reports and incidents for products. Module 4 walks through these activities, and Module 5 covers who does what.

From the record

In August 2017, FDA announced a firmware update for implanted pacemakers made by Abbott (formerly St. Jude Medical), after vulnerabilities were found that could let someone reprogram them with commercially available equipment. The update made the pacemakers accept commands only from an authorized programmer or transmitter, and needed a short in-person clinic visit. There were no reports of patient harm. The update itself carried a small clinical risk, so patients and doctors decided together whether to apply it: for devices, even a security fix is a patient-safety decision.

Practical tip

Find out who your product security team is and how to reach them before you need them. Ask early: a five-minute question during design saves weeks of rework after testing. Terms are defined in the Glossary.

Try it≈ 3 min

Trace one path from weakness to harm

Pick a product you work on, or one of the four devices. Choose one connection it has: a radio link, a network, an app, a cloud service, a service port or an update path. Then write down:

  1. The chain, one short phrase each: threat, vulnerability, security event, hazardous situation, harm.
  2. Which property is mainly at stake: confidentiality, integrity or availability.
  3. Whether the product is likely a cyber device, and why.
Compare with a worked example

Product: CardiaPace. Connection: the short-range radio link between the bedside home transmitter and the implant.

Link in the chainCardiaPace
ThreatSomeone with a home transmitter they have modified.
VulnerabilityThe implant accepts programming commands from any transmitter, without checking it is authorized.
Security eventPacing settings are changed without authorization.
Hazardous situationThe patient is paced inappropriately.
HarmInjury, or worse, for a patient who depends on the pacemaker.

Property at stake: integrity, because therapy settings were changed. If the same weakness were used to drain the implant's battery instead, it would be availability of therapy.

Cyber device? Almost certainly: manufacturer software, internet access through the home transmitter (FDA's broad reading), and a radio link that could be vulnerable. Confirm with regulatory and record the rationale.

Notice where the fix sits: an implant that obeys only authorized transmitters is a design decision, and hard to change after implant. That is Module 2.
