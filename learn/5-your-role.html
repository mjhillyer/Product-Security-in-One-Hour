Your role in product security

Product security isn't one team's job. Most security problems are caught, or missed, by people whose title doesn't say “security”. Here's who does what, when to bring in product security, and what to do when you suspect a vulnerability.

In this module

  • What each common role contributes before and after release
  • The situations that should trigger engaging product security, and how urgently
  • How to report a suspected vulnerability, and how outside researchers reach you
  • Everyday habits that keep products safe

Who does what

Your product security team sets the method, brings expertise and coordinates, but it can't see every design decision, customer conversation or supplier notice. Here is a typical split; your organization's procedure defines the actual one.

RoleTypical part, before and after releaseWatch for
Product ownerMakes security a product requirement with room in roadmap and budget; prioritizes fixes; owns end-of-support decisions.Connected features added without review; “we'll fix it next release”.
Program or project managerSchedules security activities as real deliverables; plans patch releases.Security testing squeezed into the last week.
EngineeringBuilds and tests security controls; keeps the component list accurate; fixes vulnerabilities and validates updates.Debug access, default passwords or test accounts left in.
Product securitySets the method; leads threat modeling; reviews designs; runs the PSIRT and coordinates disclosure.Being brought in late as a final gate.
Quality and regulatoryBuilds security into design controls and risk management; owns the submission, complaints and regulator reporting.Complaints closed as “no fault found” without asking “could this be security?”
Clinical or medical affairsJudges the clinical impact of security risks and fixes, and whether controls fit emergencies.Controls that slow care in an emergency.
Service and supportExplains field needs such as remote access; often first to hear of odd behavior; installs updates.Shared passwords, unofficial remote tools, resetting a device before reporting.
Sales and customer-facingBring customer security requirements in early; share approved documentation; route questions and reports.Promising fix dates nobody agreed.
Supply chain and procurementBuild security into supplier contracts; pass supplier security notices on quickly.Substitute parts nobody else hears about.

Notice the pattern in the right-hand column: most problems happen at hand-offs, when information one role holds never reaches the role that needs it.

Patient safety

Whether to take FlowGuard pumps off the network, or to update a CardiaPace pacemaker, depends on what happens to patients either way. Clinical colleagues belong in the room when a security risk is assessed.

Map your own program

A RACI chart records, for each activity, who is Responsible (does the work), Accountable (owns the outcome; exactly one role), Consulted and Informed. It makes gaps visible. Start from the program RACI template.

When to engage product security

The cheapest moment to fix a security problem is before it's built, so the most useful skill outside the security team is recognizing the moments that should trigger a conversation. Your product security team would rather hear about ten things that turn out fine than miss one that doesn't. The triggers fall into three levels of urgency:

WhenWhat it covers
Now, the same dayAnything suggesting a product may be vulnerable today: a suspected vulnerability from anyone, odd field behavior (unexpected traffic, unknown accounts), a possibly security-related complaint, a supplier notice about a flaw in a component you ship. Route customer security questionnaires promptly too; they have deadlines.
Early, at the next design reviewPlanned changes: a new interface (Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, USB, cloud, app, service port), a new or upgraded third-party component, changes to logins, encryption or updates, new data or cloud services, remote service access, operating-system upgrades and end-of-support dates. Early means before the design is settled.
No needChanges that can't affect how the product resists or recovers from attack, such as brochure photos. When in doubt, ask.
Common pitfall

“It's just a minor library update.” For DermaLens, a minor version change can bring new code and dependencies, and it changes the SBOM. Small changes are quick to review; unreviewed ones are how the component list drifts out of date. The engagement checklist turns these triggers into questions for your design reviews.

Reporting a suspected vulnerability

A vulnerability is a weakness someone could exploit to cause harm. A security incident is an event in which a weakness is, or may have been, exploited. You don't need to know which you're looking at, or whether it's real. Your job is to notice and report.

1

Report right away

Use your organization's channel, usually to the PSIRT, plus the complaint process if a customer reported it. Include product, version, when, who and what was seen.

2

Don't investigate on your own

Poking at a device in use can disrupt therapy and destroy evidence.

3

Preserve information

Keep emails, screenshots and logs. Don't reset, wipe or ship back a device until the team says so.

4

Keep it confidential

Thank the reporter and say it has been passed on. Don't confirm, deny, debate or promise dates.

Patient safety first

If a patient may be affected right now, follow your complaint and adverse-event process immediately and report the security angle in parallel.

You may not hear every detail afterward: vulnerability information is need-to-know until a fix is ready. That's normal, not a sign your report was ignored.

How outside researchers reach you

Manufacturers publish a vulnerability disclosure policy (VDP) telling independent researchers how to report a flaw and what to expect. The goal is coordinated disclosure: the fix and the news arrive together. Researchers who report are helping. If one contacts you directly, thank them, point them to the policy, and pass everything to your PSIRT the same day.

From the record

In July 2025, CISA published an advisory on issues in Medtronic's MyCareLink home monitor found by outside researchers, including an exposed debug port and an account with an empty password, all needing physical access. Medtronic had begun automatic updates in June 2025, and CISA reported no known public exploitation: report, fix and advisory together.

Everyday habits

Most of product security is small, routine choices:

Ask the security question

Does this change how the product connects, what it trusts, or what data it holds?

Keep the inventory honest

Report component, version and supplier changes.

No secrets in the wrong place

Keep passwords, keys and patient data out of tickets, chat, email and code.

No “temporary” back doors

Remove or control debug ports, test accounts and default passwords before release.

Answer from approved sources

Use published security documentation. Never guess or promise a fix date.

Take field signals seriously

An odd connection or strange returned unit is worth a message. Don't reset it.

This week

Find your internal channel for reporting a suspected product vulnerability, and your company's published disclosure policy. Save both. If you can't find either, raise it with product security. Keep the Roles & When to Engage guide nearby too.

Now, at the next design review, or no need?

For each situation, decide when the team should engage product security: now (the same day), early (at the next design review or planning milestone), or no need.

  1. A hospital reports that its FlowGuard pumps are making network connections to an address it doesn't recognize.
  2. DermaLens engineers want to add an open-source library for generating PDF reports in a future release.
  3. A supplier tells procurement about a security flaw in the Wi-Fi module used in FlowGuard pumps.
  4. Marketing updates the photos in the FlowGuard product brochure.
  5. The operating system on the OrionArm console will reach end of support in three years.
  6. A service technician finds a returned CardiaPace home transmitter with a user account nobody recognizes.
  7. The OrionArm team plans a new remote screen-sharing tool for service engineers next year.
  8. A hospital tender for FlowGuard includes a security questionnaire due in two weeks.

Want to go further? Fill in one row of the program RACI template for your team, such as “Assess a vulnerability's patient-safety impact”. A typical answer: quality and regulatory accountable (they own the risk file), product security responsible, clinical and engineering consulted. Product security usually isn't accountable for safety risk or regulatory decisions.
